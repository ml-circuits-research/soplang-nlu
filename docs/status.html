What this project does

86.8%GOAL of the best strategy (spacy): share of the 1,831 benchmark messages whose circuit a judge accepts
75.5%GOAL of VSS, our own reader and the main focus
33.8%of the dataset's 183,497 messages have a circuit (62,066)

A formalizer (here called a strategy) reads one English message alone, with no context and no knowledge base, and writes its syntactic circuit in the syntax schema: the clauses, the nominals (the words that describe one thing), the relations between them and the coordinations, with every word of the message in exactly one place. The strategies are compared on one benchmark: 1,831 messages, 1% of the dataset, drawn at random. A judge reads every circuit without knowing which strategy wrote it, and accepts or rejects it. GOAL is the share of messages whose circuit is accepted (levels L8, L9 and L10, below); the aim is over 99%. Accepted circuits become the labels of the dataset, which the strategies are measured and trained on. Terms are explained in the Glossary.

An example. The message make a bar chart of the projects by name please has this circuit as its label (accepted by a judge), taken from the dataset now:

@c1 syntax.clause form=imperative verb="make" discourse="please" complement=$n1
@n1 syntax.nominal dependent="a" dependent="bar" head="chart"
@r1 syntax.relation marker="of" from=$n1 to=$n2
@n2 syntax.nominal dependent="the" head="projects"
@r2 syntax.relation attachment=ambiguous marker="by" from=$c1 to=$n3
@n3 syntax.nominal head="name"

Read line by line, each line one wire:

  • c1 clause: form=imperative; verb "make"; discourse "please"; complement → n1 ("a bar chart")
  • n1 nominal: dependent "a"; dependent "bar"; head "chart"
  • r1 relation: marker "of"; from → n1 ("a bar chart"); to → n2 ("the projects")
  • n2 nominal: dependent "the"; head "projects"
  • r2 relation: attachment=ambiguous; marker "by"; from → c1 (the clause of "make"); to → n3 ("name")
  • n3 nominal: head "name"

attachment=ambiguous marks a phrase whose place the words do not settle (here, whether "by name" goes with the making or with the projects); the judges accept either place.

The five strategies. Three are worked on; two are parked (still measured, not improved for now, owner's decision):

  • vss (main focus): our own reader. It learns classes of words (stations) from raw text with no labels; hand-written rules about English, which name only function words, then build the circuit. Node.js only.
  • spacy: an outside dependency parser (spaCy's English transformer, trained by its makers on large treebanks) plus our layer that turns its parse into a circuit. Python.
  • distilbert: a small transformer (DistilBERT, pretrained on English text) fine-tuned on our circuits. Python with PyTorch.
  • prolog (parked): a hand-written grammar in Prolog.
  • z3 (parked): a hand-written grammar written as constraints and solved by the Z3 solver.

Why VSS is the focus, although spaCy scores higher. VSS needs only Node.js, its model is one file of 3.3 MB, it reads a message in 0.42 ms (median) on one CPU core, and it learns from raw text, so it is a reader we own and can extend, and it could run in a browser once a small loader is written. spaCy needs Python and a transformer of 500.7 MB and takes 51 ms per message. It stays as the strongest second voice: when two strategies that read from different sources write the same circuit, the label is settled with no judge (Agreement).

Who the judges are. Language-model agents (claude-opus-5-5: 11,984 circuits read so far) that follow written rules (tests/benchmark/syntax/rules.md). How often two judges, or a judge and a person, agree on this schema has not been measured yet.

The benchmark, live

GOAL = messages whose circuit is accepted (levels L8 + L9 + L10) ÷ all 1,831 messages of the benchmark. In the tables, the range after a GOAL is its 95% interval: with 1,831 messages, a GOAL is known to about ±2 points.

Each bar is the GOAL of one strategy. The vertical tick marks the aim, 99%.

Levels: L0 no valid circuit · L1–L6 rejected, with the judge's grade · L7 unused · L8 accepted · L9 equal to the label · L10 accepted, replacing the label.

What each level means
  • L0: no valid circuit.
  • L1–L6: the judge rejected the circuit, and the number is the grade: L1 a clause is wrong (its form or its verb); L2 a content word put in the discourse slot (the slot for words such as "please"); L3 words stand in the wrong wire, or a tie is wrong; L4 the circuit states what the message does not say; L5 a clause of the message is not written; L6 something is left untied.
  • L7: unused, since a strategy returns one circuit.
  • L8: accepted, and not the label the message had before the run.
  • L9: equal to the label the message had before the run, so no judge was needed.
  • L10: accepted by the judge in this run, and it replaces the label the message had.
  • waiting: no judge has read the circuit yet; not counted in GOAL.

In practice. VSS reads about 1,706 messages a second in one Node process on one CPU core, so the whole dataset takes about 2 min; with 20 processes on this machine's 20 cores, about 5 s (an estimate, not measured). spaCy takes about 2.8 h for the same work in one process: 94 times longer per message on average (121 times at the median). That makes spaCy the slowest step of every rerun over the whole dataset, such as the next one planned in Working on now. DistilBERT on the GPU takes about 12 min; Z3 about 5.8 h, with a long tail (90% of messages within 254 ms, 99% within 985 ms). For use while someone types: VSS's 0.4 ms is instant, spaCy's 51 ms goes unnoticed, and Z3's slow messages would be felt.

Not like for like. DistilBERT ran on the GPU and spaCy on the CPU. The PyTorch strategies (spaCy's transformer and DistilBERT) may use several CPU threads inside their one process, so "one process" favours them. Messages were sent one at a time; spaCy and DistilBERT would be faster on batches of messages (not measured). Not measured yet: DistilBERT on a CPU (it decides whether DistilBERT can run where there is no GPU), the CPU time of each strategy, and speed with batches.

Agreement between VSS and spaCy

Two strategies that read a message from different sources (VSS from raw text, spaCy from a parser trained on treebanks) and write the same circuit are very likely right. This is how most labels of the dataset were made. Same circuit: equal after the normal form (wire names and order do not matter). Same up to attachment: equal except where a phrase with a linking word ("on", "of", "for") starts, which the judges' rules let be the clause or a nominal of it (the journal says "up to the start of marked relations").

A working journal of the syntax-schema work: where things stand, what is being done now, and what could be done next. It is updated with every stage and committed with it, so the history of the plan stays in git.

Goal

Every formalization strategy of this repository writes the syntax schema (docs/syntax-schema.md) with no dependence on external knowledge worlds, from syntactic analysis only. The measure is the share of messages whose circuit a judge accepts (L8, or equal to the label: L9, or replacing it: L10). The aim is as high as possible, ideally over 99%.

Where things stand (2026-11-27, evening)

Dataset

  • webmeet-dataset/semantic/ and webmeet-dataset/syntactic/ mirror each other (238 files, at most 1,000 messages each; tools/layers.mjs check).
  • 62,066 of 183,497 messages have a syntactic circuit (33.8%): 56,934 by agreement of VSS and spaCy (up to the start of marked relations), 5,100 accepted by a judge, 32 written by a judge. The others have an empty cell.
  • A judge read a random 2% of the agreement circuits: 10 of 1,154 rejected (0.87%).
  • The syntactic files are the only store of labels; the registry (annotations/syntax/v1/judged/) keeps only what a judge read or wrote, with the reasons.

Rules added to VSS today (all checked on 8,000 accepted circuits and on the judged benchmark)

  • No adjectives or particular phrases in the word lists; learned word pairs and general rules instead.
  • Grading words, quality words before linking words, particles, naming participles, mentioned words, lists of names, comma asides by learned likeness, embedded questions tied to their verb.
  • From the calendar analysis: helping verbs and their subjects, words seen only as things are no verbs, capitals keep names, day words, "-ing" after a linking word, comma series.
  • From the diagram analysis: where a nominal ends (plural nouns, nominals after a linking word, graded forms).
  • From the drawing analysis (first part): the frame fixes the verb (modals, "like"), embedded questions state.

Measured and dropped

  • Counting word kinds (verb, thing, quality) also from the accepted circuits: changes 41 of 1,831 benchmark circuits with no net gain. Word counts alone do not help; the tagging of words in context does (see the lever trials below).
  • Relating "goes" to "go" in the stem rule: the retrained model shifted unrelated readings; dropped.
  • Families of strategies that read the same parse agree with each other on 77–100% of messages and repeat the same faults: agreement needs different sources.

    • The faults left are a long tail: 17 rule groups of 5 to 32 messages each (of about 520 rejected); five have measured prototypes (sentence ties, causative verb + object + bare verb, compound nouns, the opening command verb, participles after a comma).

Correction: the tagging of words matters much more than first thought.

Evidence from a better tagger is a real lever; to keep VSS portable it would be distilled into a table VSS reads, with no model at run time.

Where they differ (1,218 messages), both circuits were accepted in 515: the schema allows several readings (which word heads a name, a number after a noun as dependent or as its own nominal, modifier or discourse), and the agreement key counts them as different. A wider key that treats the variations rules.md allows as the same could settle many more messages without a judge; an agent is building and measuring it (false agreement checked on the judged pairs where only one circuit was accepted).

VSS is now above DistilBERT (73.1%); spaCy leads with 86.8%.

Ideas not yet tried (with the reason to expect a gain)

  • Supervised station model for VSS. The station tagger is learned by EM on raw text; 62,000 accepted circuits now say, for each word, its role. Mapping circuits back to stations and using their counts as a prior for the emissions and transitions could fix the verb/noun decisions behind most L1 faults. Risk: the stations are finer than the syntax keys; the mapping needs care.
  • Learned decisions in the VSS linker. Which earlier thing a listed thing joins, where a relation starts, the form of a clause: today these are rules with thresholds. Small classifiers over the features the rules already use, trained on the accepted circuits, could replace the thresholds.
  • Reranking VSS readings. VSS can produce several station paths (k-best); a scorer trained on accepted circuits could pick among them. Measured earlier on the nlu schema: alternatives contained an accepted reading for only 3–6% of rejected messages, so this needs the linker to produce alternatives too.
  • DistilBERT as a voice inside VSS. Use the DistilBERT prediction as a feature where VSS's rules are unsure (for example a word that can be noun or verb).
  • Retrain DistilBERT after each large batch of labels, always holding out the benchmark messages.
  • A fresh benchmark sample once the labels have grown, since the current sample's messages now have labels and are held out of every training.
  • Carry the VSS rules into Z3 later, if a fourth independent voice is needed (owner's remark).

Making VSS reusable (owner's question of 2026-11-27: general rules or particular ones?)

The rules added so far are statements about English that name only function words and use what VSS learns from text; the analysis was split by area (drawing, diagram, calendar) only to find the faults where they occur. What is not yet general: some thresholds measured on this dataset (a word seen as a thing at least 20 times, the likeness 0.3), the choice of which constructions to cover (driven by the frequent faults of commands to a drawing application), and a few borderline legend words (left/right as direction adverbs, today/tomorrow, thanks). The steps that would make VSS a reusable engine with a standard grammar:

  • Audit of generality, after the current batch of rules: agents list every rule and word list of VSS and try to show each one particular; each is classed as general English, a threshold tuned on this data, or particular. Particular ones are rewritten or removed.
  • Rules as data. Move the rules out of JavaScript into constructions declared in the legend (SOP Lang), over stations and word marks: the engine stays generic, the grammar of English becomes a file that can be read, checked and extended.
  • Thresholds learned, not set. The code names the features of each decision; the weights are learned from the accepted circuits (as DistilBERT learns), so no number is tuned by hand on this dataset.
  • A test outside the dataset. Run VSS on English it was never tuned on (other kinds of messages or a public corpus) and have a judge read a sample, to measure how general it is.

Glossary

Message
One text a user typed, such as "make a bar chart of the projects by name please". The dataset holds messages to a drawing app, a diagram tool, a calendar and a document editor.
Strategy (formalizer)
A program that reads one message alone, with no context and no knowledge base, and writes its circuit. Five are measured: vss, spacy, distilbert, prolog, z3 (what each is).
VSS
The name of our own reader (strategies/vss/). The repository does not expand the letters; its word vectors are those of vector symbolic architectures (How VSS works).
Circuit
The syntactic analysis of one message, written as lines of SOP Lang, one line (a wire) per part. Every word of the message has exactly one place in it. There is an example under What this project does.
Wire
One line of a circuit: a name, a type, and keys that hold words of the message or point to other wires. Example: @n1 syntax.nominal dependent="the" head="box".
The four wire types
clause: one verb with everything that belongs to it (one command, statement or question). nominal: the words that describe one thing ("the two small red boxes"). relation: a tie between two wires, usually through a linking word ("on", "of", "next to"). coordination: things or clauses put side by side with "and", "or", "but".
Syntax schema
The rules that say which wires and keys exist and how to use them: 4 wire types and 18 keys (syntax-schema.md).
SOP Lang
The line language circuits are written in: each line starts with a name such as @c1, then a type and keys with their values. The project writes its other declarations in it too, such as the VSS legend.
Judge
A reviewer that reads a message and one circuit, without knowing which strategy wrote it, and accepts it or rejects it with a grade (L1 to L6), following written rules (tests/benchmark/syntax/rules.md). Here the judges are language-model agents; the registry names each one. How often two judges, or a judge and a person, agree on this schema has not been measured yet.
Packet
A file of up to 200 circuits that one judge reads in one go (judging/packet-N.jsonl in a run folder); the verdicts go to judging/verdicts-N.jsonl. "Judged in packets 45-46" names two such files.
Label
The accepted circuit of a message, kept in the sop column of the dataset's syntactic files. Labels are what strategies are measured against and trained on.
Gold and silver labels
Gold: labels a judge accepted or wrote. Silver: labels settled by agreement, which DistilBERT trains on at a lower weight.
Agreement
Two strategies that read from different sources wrote the same circuit for a message. Such a circuit is taken as the label with no judge; a judge reads a random 2% as a control.
Audit
The random share (2%) of the circuits settled by agreement that a judge reads all the same, to measure how often agreement is wrong.
Families of strategies
Strategies that read the same source, for example several variants built on the same spaCy parse, form one family. They agree with each other on 77–100% of messages and repeat the same faults, so agreement counts only between families.
Normal form (core)
A circuit rewritten so that the names of its wires and the order of its lines do not matter. Two circuits with the same normal form, called their core, are the same circuit.
Up to attachment
Two circuits that are equal except for where a phrase with a linking word starts (the clause or a nominal of it); the journal calls it "up to the start of marked relations". The judges' rules allow either place, so such circuits count as agreeing. attachment=ambiguous marks a phrase whose place the words do not settle.
Registry
Every circuit a judge has read, accepted or rejected, with the reason (webmeet-dataset/annotations/syntax/v1/judged). A circuit is judged once. It also keeps a few agreement circuits with no judge, as accepted readings.
Round (conversion round)
One pass over the next files of the dataset: the strategies write circuits, agreement settles what it can, a judge reads the rest, and the accepted circuits are written into the files.
Benchmark
1% of the dataset's messages drawn at random (2026-11-27-syntax-benchmark-random, 1,831 messages). Every circuit of every strategy on it is judged. Its messages are held out of training on labels; VSS learns its stations from raw text that includes them, without labels. Some rules were found by reading its rejected circuits, so it is no longer an unseen sample; a fresh sample is planned.
Levels and GOAL
L0 no valid circuit; L1–L6 rejected, with the judge's grade; L7 unused; L8 accepted; L9 equal to the earlier label; L10 accepted and replacing the earlier label. GOAL is the share of messages at L8, L9 or L10.
95% interval
The range in which the true share most likely lies, given how many messages were measured (the Wilson score interval). On 1,831 messages it is about ±2 points, so a difference of 1 or 2 points between two separate measurements can be chance.
Station (VSS)
A class of words that VSS learns from raw text by the company words keep and by their order, with no labels. VSS reads a message as a path through stations; then its rules build the circuit.
EM (expectation–maximization)
How VSS's station tagger is trained: it guesses which station each word stands at, counts, and guesses again, round after round, from raw text with no labels.
Linker (VSS)
The part of VSS that builds clauses, relations and coordinations from the words and their stations, by rules with thresholds, in one pass.
Drawing, diagram and calendar analyses
Studies of the faults VSS makes on the files of one area of the dataset: agents read a sample of VSS circuits and proposed general rules, which were then added to VSS.
Lever trial
An experiment on a copy of VSS that adds one learned aid, judged against the same copy without it on the same benchmark messages. A lever is a change beyond adding single rules.
Station prior
A lever trial: VSS's station tagger trained again with the accepted circuits mapped to stations, as a weighted starting point.
DistilBERT evidence
A lever trial: a per-word table learned from DistilBERT's circuits says how a word is used (verb, word of a nominal, modifier, marker, other). Where the table is sure and VSS disagrees, VSS keeps the word off the stations the table rules out. "On the verb" uses it for the verb only; "on roles" for all five uses.
Checkpoint
The saved weights of a trained model; syntax-v2 is the DistilBERT checkpoint measured now.
Epoch
One pass of training over all training examples.
Development messages
Labelled messages kept out of training to choose settings and check progress while training. They are not the benchmark.
Held out
Kept out of training on labels, so that a measurement on those messages is fair.
Parked
Still measured on the benchmark, but not improved for now (owner's decision): prolog and z3.
