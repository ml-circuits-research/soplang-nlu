How syntactic labels are made and measured

Written 2026-11-27. Revised the same evening after two reviews; folders and files are described as they stood at about 18:00.

Related documents: The VSS reader: architecture (how VSS reads a message, step by step, and every rule it holds) and Decisions and insights of the syntax work, 2026-11-27 (what was decided that day and why, what was measured, what is open).

The syntactic/ layer of webmeet-dataset gives each message one syntactic analysis, written in the syntax schema (syntax-schema.md). This document explains how such an analysis becomes the reference annotation (the "label") of its message and how the programs that write analyses are measured against those labels: the levels L0 to L10 and GOAL, the language-model judges and their rules, the benchmark on a random 1% of the dataset, the conversion rounds, agreement between programs, the batch conversion of the whole dataset, where every file lives, and the pitfalls seen so far. A reviewer can use it to check the method; a newcomer can use it to run each step. The reader that most of the work was about, VSS, is described in vss-architecture.html; the decisions and findings of the day are in syntax-work-2026-11-27.html. Every statement about code names its file, relative to the root of soplang-nlu unless it starts with webmeet-dataset/. Every number says what it counts and where it was counted.

1. At a glance, with a timeline

A formalizer reads one English message and writes one circuit: lines of SOP Lang, the project's line notation, that split the message into clauses and nominals (noun phrases), give every word one place and tie the parts (section 3). The code also calls a formalizer a strategy; both words mean the same program. The label of a message is an accepted circuit. It is stored in the message's row of webmeet-dataset/syntactic/<tab>/<file>.csv and nowhere else. A circuit can be accepted in three ways, and the accepted_by cell of the row records which:

  • judge: a judge (a language model working by tests/benchmark/syntax/rules.md) read the circuit and accepted it;
  • written: a judge wrote the circuit because no formalizer's circuit was accepted;
  • agreement: formalizers that read the message from different sources wrote circuits that count as alike, and no judge read it. "Alike" means equal cores (section 4) in the hand-run rounds, and equal relaxed cores, which ignore where a phrase with a linking word attaches, in the batch conversion that produced almost all agreement labels (section 10).

The same tools serve two purposes. A benchmark measures formalizers on a fixed sample and prints a table of levels. A conversion round writes the syntactic files of the dataset, and reports the same table along the way. In the owner's words, testing and converting are one activity (owner's decision, kept in tests/benchmark/syntax/README.md, section "A conversion round").

The path of one circuit through the tools:

  1. A strategy writes the circuit. tests/benchmark/syntax/run.mjs runs every strategy over the sample, each in a process of its own. No valid circuit: level L0. Validity is checked by validateSyntax in lib/syntax.mjs.
  2. The circuit is put in normal form and its core is computed. normalForm in tests/benchmark/syntax/lib.mjs. Two circuits of one message are the same when their cores are equal.
  3. Is it the label of the message? Then it is accepted already and no judge reads it. Against the labels frozen before the run, this is level L9.
  4. Does the registry of judge decisions hold it? Then it keeps the verdict it has, accepted or rejected. webmeet-dataset/annotations/syntax/v1/judged/*.jsonl
  5. In a conversion round only: did strategies of enough different families write it alike? Then it is settled by agreement. A random share of these circuits still goes to a judge as a control.
  6. Otherwise it goes to a judge in a packet, once per message and core, with nothing that tells who wrote it. The judge grades it L8 (acceptable) or L1 to L6 (rejected, with the reason).
  7. Publication. What the judge decided goes into a new registry file. Then the labels: judge-publish.mjs (benchmark) makes every accepted circuit the label of its message, replacing what the row held. round.mjs write (round) writes new syntactic files and fills empty cells, but never replaces a circuit already in a file (section 8).
  8. The table. table.mjs (or round.mjs report) counts, per strategy, how many messages stand at each level, and GOAL.

The strategies

tests/benchmark/syntax/strategies.mjs lists five strategies in strategyModules; these are the five rows of the benchmark table. The owner's decision limits the work to four: VSS, one strategy that reads the dependency parse, DistilBERT, and one hand-written grammar. Prolog and Z3 both hold that fourth place until one of them is dropped (comment in strategies.mjs). "Parked" means they still run in the benchmark but are no longer improved.

Eight further strategies are listed in retiredModules: they are left out of rounds and benchmark, still run by name with run.mjs --only, and their code is kept. The batch conversion used one of them, spacy-direct.

Grammar terms used below, in plain words: a clause is one verb with what belongs to it; a nominal is the group of words that describe one thing (noun phrase); its head is the main word and the others are its dependents; a marker is the linking word of a relation (a preposition, "if", the "to" of an infinitive); attachment is the question of which word a phrase hangs from; coordination is putting things side by side with "and", "or", "but". A dependency parse is the tree a statistical parser such as spaCy builds, with one head for each word.

3. The circuit that is judged

The schema is syntax, version 3, declared in schemas/syntax/schema.sop and specified in syntax-schema.md. It has four wire types and 18 keys (17 names, since negation stands on two types). It replaced the earlier nlu schema, which had more than 50 keys (owner's decision, commit 8536c9c and later).

A program checks the form before any judge sees a circuit: validateSyntax(text, sop) in lib/syntax.mjs. Every word of the message must stand in exactly one quoted value, and punctuation in none. When a word occurs more than once, values take its occurrences in the order the circuit is written (Rule 1 of the specification). A relation that leaves open whether a phrase hangs from the verb or from a nominal starts at the clause and carries attachment=ambiguous (Rule 2).

4. When two circuits are the same: normal form and core

Every comparison in the tools is a comparison of cores: whether a circuit is the label, whether the registry holds it, whether two strategies agree. The core is computed in two steps, both in tests/benchmark/syntax/lib.mjs.

The normal form (normalForm) rewrites a circuit so that:

  • wires are named c, n, r, k with a number, in the order of their first word;
  • dependent, modifier, auxiliary, negation and discourse hold one word per value;
  • marker and conjunction hold neighbouring words as one value;
  • a clause lists its subject, then its complements; a relation lists from, then to.

The core is coreOf(text, normalForm) from lib/syntax.mjs: a sorted list of lines. Positions count the words of the message from 0, punctuation left out. A wire is named by the position of its first word, and a relation by (start>end), the names of its from and to wires. Span lines (3-4 marker @(0>5)) say which words a value holds, under which key, in which wire. The other lines give a wire's references (clause 0 complement 1: the complement of clause 0 is the nominal that starts at word 1) and its unquoted values (clause 0 form imperative). Wire names and line order no longer matter.

# put the cat next to the red box
# strategy A
@c1 syntax.clause form=imperative verb="put" complement=$n1
@n1 syntax.nominal dependent="the" head="cat"
@r1 syntax.relation from=$c1 to=$n2 marker="next to"
@n2 syntax.nominal dependent="the" dependent="red" head="box"
# strategy B
@thing syntax.nominal head="cat" dependent="the"
@place syntax.nominal dependent="the red" head="box"
@link syntax.relation marker="next" marker="to" to=$place from=$act
@act syntax.clause complement=$thing verb="put" form=imperative

# both have this normal form
@c1 syntax.clause form=imperative verb="put" complement=$n1
@n1 syntax.nominal dependent="the" head="cat"
@r1 syntax.relation marker="next to" from=$c1 to=$n2
@n2 syntax.nominal dependent="the" dependent="red" head="box"

# and this core
0-0 verb @0
1-1 dependent @1
2-2 head @1
3-4 marker @(0>5)
5-5 dependent @5
6-6 dependent @5

A constructed example: the two circuits are from tests/syntax-benchmark.test.mjs ("the normal form is one text for one circuit, whoever wrote it"). The normal form and core were printed with normalForm on 2026-11-27.

Why it is done this way

  • The judge must not see who wrote a circuit. Wire names such as @x1 or @thing would tell. In normal form, a circuit written by several strategies reaches the judge once and looks the same whoever wrote it.
  • coreOf alone tells apart circuits that say the same thing: marker="next to" against marker="next" marker="to", or from=$a to=$b against to=$b from=$a. The test checks that the two raw cores above differ and the two normal forms are equal.
  • Packets, the registry and the syntactic files all hold circuits in normal form, so the same circuit always has the same text.

Not obvious from the code

  • attachment=ambiguous is part of the core. The same circuit with and without the mark counts as two circuits. Only the relaxed comparison of section 10 joins them.
  • When repeated words cross between wires, the normal form sometimes cannot be written. normalForm then keeps the core of the circuit as written (kept: 'as written'). Such a circuit may fail to match the same reading written in another order. How often this happens was not measured. syntax-schema.md (Rule 1, "Known limit") reports that the writer meets the crossing case in 2 to 8 of 1,831 messages, depending on the formalizer.
  • The registry stores a core field, but the tools never trust it. readRegistry (tests/benchmark/syntax/lib.mjs) computes the core again from text and sop, so a change of coreOf is picked up without rewriting the registry.
  • The core of a label is computed only when it is asked for (labelLine, same file). So reading all 183,497 rows of the syntactic files costs no core computation until a run looks up its own messages.

5. The levels L0 to L10 and GOAL

The table has one row per strategy and puts each message at one level. It is written by table.mjs for a benchmark run and by round.mjs report for a round. Both call tableOf and levelOf in tests/benchmark/syntax/lib.mjs.

LevelMeaning
L0The strategy returned no valid circuit.
L1 to L6The circuit is rejected, by a judge of this run or already in the registry. The level is the judge's grade (section 6).
L7Unused. In the earlier scale of levels it meant one good and one bad reading; a strategy now returns one circuit.
L8Accepted, but neither L9 nor L10: accepted earlier in the registry; accepted in this run, by a judge or by agreement, for a message that had no label before the run; or made the label after the run started.
L9The circuit is equal to the label the message had before the run. No judge was needed.
L10Accepted in this run, by a judge or by agreement, for a message that had another label before the run.
waitingNo judge has read the circuit yet.

Grades and levels share their numbers. A judge's grade L8 becomes level L8 or L10, depending on whether the message had a label before the run. L1 to L6 are checked by the judge in order and the first that applies is given, so the number names the first kind of fault found, not how bad the circuit is.

How levelOf decides, in order

  1. No valid circuit (no core): L0.
  2. The core equals the core of the label before the run: L9. This is checked before any verdict.
  3. The run itself has a verdict for the circuit: a judge's line in judging/verdicts-<k>.jsonl, or, in a conversion round, settlement by agreement (key/agreement.jsonl, counted as a grade L8). If rejected, the grade. If accepted: L10 when the message had a label before the run, else L8.
  4. The registry holds the circuit: accepted gives L8, rejected gives its grade.
  5. The circuit equals the label of now, because it became the label after the run started (for example, settled by agreement in a later round): L8.
  6. Otherwise: waiting.

GOAL = (L8 + L9 + L10) / all messages of the run. The GOAL_LEVELS constant in lib.mjs lists the three levels. While circuits wait, GOAL is a lower bound: a waiting message counts in the denominator only, so GOAL can only rise as waiting circuits receive verdicts. tableOf also gives atMost, the upper bound, which is GOAL if every waiting circuit were accepted, and counts the accepted circuits that carry attachment=ambiguous. The owner set the goal on 2026-11-27: GOAL ideally over 99%. The owner refused to restate it as "precision on unflagged readings plus coverage"; the dataset is to be corrected through the judged loop until the comparison with it is exact (owner's decision).

Why it is done this way

GOAL counts judged acceptance, not exact match with the label. Several circuits of one message can be acceptable under rules.md (for example, a phrase with a marker may start at the clause or at a nominal). The label is one of them, chosen by chooseLabels (tests/benchmark/syntax/lib.mjs: the one most strategies wrote, then the lowest item number). Exact match with the label would therefore count acceptable circuits as wrong, so every circuit that differs from the label is judged. One consequence: L9 rewards the strategy whose acceptable circuit happened to become the label; see the box in section 7 for what that does to the random benchmark.

The test "levels and GOAL from the labels, the registry and the verdicts of the run" in tests/syntax-benchmark.test.mjs fixes the procedure on ten constructed messages:

The labels before the run

L9 and L10 compare with the labels the messages had before the run, not with the labels of now. keepLabelsBefore in lib.mjs writes them to <run>/key/labels-before.jsonl. It does this the first time judge-prepare.mjs, round.mjs settle or round.mjs write is called for the run, or judge-publish.mjs when it has something to publish (it stops at "Nothing new to publish" before the call), and never changes the file after that. A line is {"text", "label", "source", "accepted_by"} for each message of the run that had a label. Older runs have a file in an earlier format, which holds every label of the dataset with the keys {text, label, core, run, source, previous} and no accepted_by. In eval-results/2026-11-27-syntax-benchmark-random/key/labels-before.jsonl that is 60,914 lines, of which 593 belong to the sample; readLabelsBefore reads both formats. A run with no such file is compared with the labels of now.

Why it is done this way

A publication changes labels (section 7). Without frozen labels, the table of an earlier run would change after every later publication. For example, a strategy whose circuit was the label would drop from L9 to "waiting" once a judge accepted another strategy's circuit for the same message, because the old label may have no registry line at all. This is what the session notes recorded after the benchmark publication replaced agreement labels: the VSS circuits equal to the old labels no longer appeared accepted anywhere (syntax-work-2026-11-27.html, I6).

Not obvious from the code

  • L9 is decided before any verdict. A circuit equal to the label before the run is L9 even if the registry holds a rejection of it.
  • In a round, a circuit settled by agreement for a message that already had another label counts as L10, though no judge read it. tests/benchmark/syntax/README.md describes L10 as "a judge accepted"; the round section of the same file says the agreement case too. Such an L10 reports a replacement that round.mjs write may not perform (section 8).
  • An audited agreed circuit counts as accepted by agreement (L8 or L10) until its verdict arrives, because runVerdicts (lib.mjs) adds every agreed circuit as a grade L8 unless a judge's verdict replaces it. It never shows as "waiting".
  • A label is never sent to a judge again. collectItems (lib.mjs) leaves out every circuit equal to the label, and agreedCircuits leaves out every circuit that is the label already. Labels settled by agreement are therefore trusted until a publication replaces them. The only check on them is the audit (section 10).
  • Verdict lines with a format problem: what still counts is described in section 6, "The verdict file and its check". table.mjs prints how many problem lines there are.

6. The judges

The external reviewer proposed the judging criterion and the owner adopted it (owner's decision). A circuit passes if the next layer, the semantic reading that later takes the circuit with context and decides what the message means, can continue from it by completing it. It fails only when it states something wrong or loses explicit information. Plausible and incomplete readings pass. The rules are in tests/benchmark/syntax/rules.md. writeJudging in lib.mjs copies them, with the check script, into the judging folder of a run as RULES.md and check.mjs; round.mjs rewrite-prepare and pool.mjs collect copy rules.md the same way. Every new call copies it again (see Pitfalls).

What a judge reads

  • judging/RULES.md, before every packet and again in the middle of a long one;
  • one judging/packet-<k>.jsonl. Each line is {"n", "text", "sop"}: the item number, the message and one circuit in normal form. Nothing tells which strategy wrote the circuit. Which strategies wrote item n is kept in key/items.jsonl, which is not for the judge.

The benchmark tools do not start judges. In the work of 2026-11-27, judges were language-model agents; the registry lines name the model claude-opus-5-5. The instruction each agent received beyond RULES.md and its packet, and how packets were assigned to agents, are not recorded in the repository.

The rule, in one question

Can the next layer continue from this circuit by completing it, or must it first undo a wrong statement? A rejection must quote the words of the message and name the wrong statement or the lost information. rules.md says: "If you cannot quote the words, the circuit is acceptable." Each line is judged by itself. Several circuits of one message can be acceptable, and a circuit is never compared with the one the judge would have written.

What must be right (from rules.md):

  1. the split into clauses and the form of each;
  2. the verb of each clause, or that it has none, with its auxiliary and negation words;
  3. whether a fragment shares the verb of another clause (ellipsis);
  4. which words form each nominal, and its head;
  5. the subject when the words show one, and which nominals are complements of which clause;
  6. the end of every relation, its marker, and its start when the words settle it;
  7. the conjuncts of every coordination.

Where several readings pass: the start of a relation with a marker (the clause, or any nominal of it that grammar allows, with or without attachment=ambiguous); which earlier wire a coordination joins when grammar allows several; a word that can be a noun or a verb; a misspelt or unknown word. What is not judged: what a complement or modifier means for the verb, which clause holds a discourse word, the order and names of wires, whether a question is meant as a request, what a pronoun refers to, and anything only context or world knowledge would settle.

Grades

A judge gives L8, or the first of L1 to L6 that applies.

GradeWhen
L8Acceptable.
L1A clause is wrong: its form, its verb, an auxiliary or negation in the wrong place, words of one clause held in another, a missing or wrong ellipsis.
L2A word with content is held in discourse, so its place in the sentence is lost.
L3Words in the wrong wire or a wrong tie: a nominal split or merged, a wrong head, a wrong or swapped subject, a complement of the wrong clause, a relation with a wrong end, a marker that is no linking word, or a start the words rule out, a linking word held as a modifier or dependent, a wrong conjunct.
L4The circuit states what the message does not say: an ellipsis, subject, relation or coordination the words do not give.
L5A clause is not written: its verb is held as a modifier, dependent, head or discourse word.
L6Something is left untied inside its sentence, or a pairing the words give is not written ("x 120" with "120" tied to nothing).

In the first judging pass of the random benchmark the judges graded 5,858 circuits. 3,115 were accepted. Of the 2,743 rejected, 1,640 were L3, 834 L1, 115 L5, 111 L6, 31 L2 and 12 L4 (counted over eval-results/2026-11-27-syntax-benchmark-random/judging/verdicts-1.jsonl to verdicts-35.jsonl).

Settled cases

The section "Settled cases" of rules.md holds 29 cases: six from conversion round 1 (commit 01695d3), three from the benchmark judges (commit a81dd93, 17:38), and the rest from earlier in the day: those written when rules.md was created (commit e166870), whose criterion and first cases came from the judging rules of the nlu schema (strategies/vss/judging-rules.md, where commits 615e232 and 9196fe7 had added them), and five added in commit 24de853 (per syntax-work-2026-11-27.html, "Where the rules came from"). They were added wherever judges ruled differently on the same construction or asked. The section "What you write" follows them. A few of the cases:

  • "Could you move it?" is one interrogative clause: "Could" is an auxiliary and "you" the subject.
  • A message with no verb ("pie radius smaller", "legend on the right") is a fragment. Making a noun its verb is L1.
  • A command needs its verb. When the first word of a message with no other verb can be a noun or a verb ("book design review"), it is the verb. Held as a noun, the command is lost: L1.
  • A number or name right after a noun ("block 2", "x 120") may be a dependent of that noun, or a nominal of its own tied to the noun by a relation with no marker. Both pass. Tied to nothing, it is L6.
  • Words said to the listener that are quoted as content ("a paragraph saying Thank you") are content. Held as discourse, they are L2.
  • A clause that opens with a question word inside another clause ("tell me where it is", "show what I said") states: its form is declarative, and it is tied to the clause it belongs to. Written as interrogative it is L1. A clause that asks at the start of a sentence ("where is it?") is interrogative. (Added in a81dd93.)

Not obvious from the code

  • The quote check (quotesTheMessage) is loose. It looks only at single quote marks: the straight ' and the curly ‘ and ’ (QUOTE = /['‘’]/). Any two of them form a pair, and an apostrophe counts as one; double quotes do not count. The test passes when the text between some pair, lower-cased with spaces evened out, occurs in the message and holds a letter or digit. It shows that the note points at words of the message. It does not show that the note is right.
  • When the first line for an n fails the check, a second line for the same n is not reported as "judged twice" and can count.
  • Two judge agents read all their circuits and then wrote all-accept verdict files with a script. An automatic safety check flagged both. The session checked only that the two agents had changed nothing but their verdict files (session notes, recorded in syntax-work-2026-11-27.html, I11; not recounted here). A verdict file written in one go is no sign by itself that circuits went unread, but that check does not show the all-accept verdicts to be right either, and no spot-check of those packets is recorded. A reviewer who doubts a packet can re-read a sample of its L8 lines against the rules.
  • Each circuit was read by one judge. Whether two judges agree, and whether a judge agrees with a human annotator, was not measured (section 17).

Publication: accepted circuits become labels

judge-publish.mjs does three things:

  • Registry. It writes one new file <tag>.jsonl into webmeet-dataset/annotations/syntax/v1/judged/, with one line per circuit judged in the run. It never overwrites a file and never adds a second line for a circuit the registry already holds.
  • Labels. When several circuits of one message were accepted, the label is the one most strategies wrote, and among equals the one with the lowest item number n (chooseLabels in lib.mjs). writeLabels (same file) rewrites only the files that change, and in them only the cells sop, source, accepted_by of those rows. Row order, line ends and message cells stay byte for byte, so the layers check still passes. Nothing is committed: committing the dataset is a separate step.
  • Replaced agreement labels (code of 17:45). An agreement label that the publication replaces, and that the registry does not hold, is written as an accepted line with judge "agreement" into <tag>-replaced-agreement.jsonl, so the circuit stays recorded as an acceptable reading. The publication of the random benchmark ran before this code; its file was added afterwards (section 16).

8. Conversion rounds

A conversion round gives one or more files of webmeet-dataset/semantic/ their counterpart in syntactic/, and measures the strategies on them (owner's decision). The tool is tests/benchmark/syntax/round.mjs, with one sub-command per step. <round> is a new folder under eval-results/. Every command takes --dataset <folder>. Without it, the commands after start use the dataset recorded in the round's files.json, and plan and start use ../webmeet-dataset resolved from the soplang-nlu folder (packageRoot in lib.mjs), not from the working directory. sample.mjs and drop-duplicates.mjs resolve their default the same way.

  1. plan [--rounds 3]: shows which files the next rounds would take. Writes nothing.
  2. start <round> [--min-messages 500] [--order size|name] [--files a.csv,…]: takes the source file with the fewest messages that has no syntactic file and belongs to no round in progress, and adds more until the round has at least 500 messages (pickFiles in round.mjs). It writes files.json, with a sha256 of each source as read from disk, and sample.json, with each distinct non-empty text once and ids <tab>/<file>#<record>.
  3. run.mjs <round> --sample <round>/sample.json: as in a benchmark.
  4. settle <round>: decides what needs no judge and prepares packets for the rest (options below).
  5. Judges write judging/verdicts-<k>.jsonl.
  6. rewrite-prepare <round> [--now]: packets for messages with no accepted circuit (section 9).
  7. write <round> --tag <name> --judge <model> [--allow-open]: publishes and writes the syntactic files. Without --judge the registry lines say "judge": "unknown", with no warning (judge-publish.mjs prints one).
  8. report <round>: how the messages are settled, how often agreement was right, and the table of levels. Can be called at any time.

settle: what it decides

For every message, the circuits of the strategies are grouped by core:

  • a circuit the registry holds keeps its verdict;
  • a circuit equal to the label is accepted already;
  • a circuit that strategies of at least --families different families wrote alike (3 by default) is settled by agreement (agreedCircuits in lib.mjs);
  • a random share of the agreed circuits (--audit, 0.05 by default) still goes to a judge, mixed with the others so the judge cannot tell which they are. When the judge rejects one, the judge's word counts;
  • the remaining circuits go to packets, as in a benchmark, according to --judge.

It writes settle.json (counts) and key/agreement.jsonl (the agreed circuits, each with an audit flag). The flag is set on every agreed circuit that stands in the run's items, whatever sent it there: the random share, --focus, --judge all, or an earlier call (item.audit = true for any agreed item in settle of round.mjs). For example, in round 1, 262 of the 263 agreed circuits were flagged because they were already in packets from an earlier call (settle.json: 262 agreed and sent to a judge, 1,798 sent in all).

write: what it publishes

Into the registry file <tag>.jsonl it publishes only what a judge decided: each circuit a judge read, including audited agreed ones, and each circuit a judge wrote. A circuit settled by agreement that no judge read gets no registry line. Its only record is its row in the syntactic file, with accepted_by "agreement". A round in which no judge decided anything writes no registry file.

Then it writes each syntactic file of the round. Every message of the source file appears once, in order, with the columns text, sop, source, accepted_by. The circuit of a message is its label if the label is among the accepted circuits, else the accepted circuit most strategies wrote (chooseCircuit in round.mjs). The command writes nothing, and lists the messages, while any message of a new file has no circuit. --allow-open writes empty cells for them instead. An existing file is only completed: if the messages are the same and in the same order, and every circuit already there is kept, its empty cells are filled. Otherwise the file is left as it is.

Not obvious from the code

  • round.mjs write never changes a cell that holds a circuit, and writeLabels is never called in round.mjs. For an existing syntactic file, if the round would change any circuit already there, the whole file is left untouched ("exists already: not written"), including its empty cells. The accepted circuits of such messages reach only the registry. So the owner's decision that every circuit a judge accepts becomes the label holds for judge-publish.mjs, not for rounds. In a round on an existing file, an L10 (also one by agreement) reports a replacement that write does not perform.
  • The registry lines, the round's settled circuits and the labels can disagree about a circuit. roundState (round.mjs) applies the sources in an order where the later one overrides; from strongest to weakest: the registry, a judge of this round, the label, agreement in this round, a circuit written by a judge in this round. The code comment says only "the strongest word last". The order fits two rules of the tools: the first registry line for a circuit counts, and a circuit the registry holds is never sent to a judge again, so a judge of the round can disagree with the registry only if the registry took the circuit up after the packets were made.
  • report.md is a snapshot of when it was called. The round-1 report in its folder still says "8 need a judge to write the circuit", because it was written before write. The round-2 report says 33 are open for the same reason. written.json is the record of what was published.

9. Writing a circuit when none is accepted

In a conversion round, a message whose circuits were all rejected, or that no strategy wrote a valid circuit for, has no accepted circuit. round.mjs rewrite-prepare <round> puts such messages into rewriting/packet-<k>.jsonl, 100 to a packet by default. Each line is {"n", "text", "rejected": [{"sop", "note"}]}, so the writer sees what went wrong. The writer is a judge in the role of fixer. It reads rewriting/REWRITE.md (from tests/benchmark/syntax/rewrite.md), RULES.md and the schema, and writes written-<k>.jsonl with lines {"n", "sop"}.

node check.mjs <k> in that folder calls tests/benchmark/syntax/rewrite-check.mjs. It validates each circuit against the schema and the message with validateSyntax and says in plain words what to correct ("these words of the message stand in no value: …"). It also refuses a circuit equal to one already rejected for that message.

A message that still has a circuit waiting for a judge is left out, because that circuit may yet be accepted. --now takes it anyway. This is needed after settle --judge needed, which never sends the other strategies' circuits to a judge.

Not obvious from the code

A written circuit is accepted with no second judge. round.mjs write records it in the registry with source "judge", verdict "accepted" and the note "written by the judge: no circuit of a formalizer was accepted for this message". The label gets accepted_by "written" only when no formalizer's circuit of the message is accepted (section 8). Rounds 1 and 2 wrote 8 and 33 circuits this way (published.written in eval-results/2026-11-27-round-0{1,2}/written.json), of which 7 and 25 became labels. Relaxed agreement

The judges accept several starts for a relation with a marker (section 6). Two strategies that differ only in where such a phrase attaches would never agree under exact matching, though both circuits pass. relaxedCore in lib.mjs therefore removes the start of every relation that has a marker, and its attachment=ambiguous line, before comparing. That covers prepositional phrases and also clauses introduced by a marker such as "if" or the "to" of an infinitive.

# turn underline on for the Results heading   (round 1)
# vss: "for the Results heading" hangs from "underline"
@r1 syntax.relation marker="for" from=$n1 to=$n2
# spacy-direct: it hangs from the clause
@r1 syntax.relation marker="for" from=$c1 to=$n2

# the relaxed core of "put the cat next to the red box": the start became *
3-4 marker @(*>5)
relation (*>5) to 5

Under --relaxed, the circuit that stands for the group is the one written by the first strategy of --prefer. The batch uses --prefer vss, so it is VSS's choice of start that becomes the label, and the parser may have chosen another start. Why VSS's circuit, and not the parser's, stands for the group is not recorded in the code or the session notes.

Why it is done this way

The owner asked for no duplicated data (owner's decision). 14. The layers check

The owner's decision: semantic/ and syntactic/ are mirrors, with the same paths, the same messages and the same order, and no file holds more than 1,000 messages. webmeet-dataset/tools/layers.mjs keeps this without any language model:

  • node tools/layers.mjs split [--max 1000] [--dry] splits each semantic file of more than --max messages into parts <name>.01.csv, <name>.02.csv, …, in row order, each part repeating the header. The bytes of every row are kept. It checks that the header followed by the rows of all parts gives back the original file before removing it. A syntactic file with the same path is split at the same rows, after a check that its messages match. Commit d44ab91 split 49 files into 187 parts.
  • node tools/layers.mjs check checks that every syntactic file has a semantic file with the same path, the same number of rows and the same message in each row. It exits with 1 and lists what differs. At 18:00 it printed: "238 of 238 semantic files have their syntactic file. Every one mirrors its source: same path, same messages, same order."

Not obvious from the code

  • The check compares messages only, never circuits. A wrong or missing sop cell passes.
  • round.mjs write writes each distinct, non-empty text of a source once (messagesOfFile in round.mjs), while the check compares row counts. A source file with a repeated or empty message would get a syntactic file the check rejects. No such file exists now, since the check passes on all 238.
  • writeLabels and round.mjs write keep row order and line ends exactly so that this check keeps passing after every publication.

16. Pitfalls

A publication replaces labels

Every circuit a judge accepts in a benchmark becomes the label of its message, also over a label settled by agreement that no judge rejected. In the random benchmark, 318 of the 593 messages that had a label before the run hold another circuit now; 307 of the old labels were agreement labels (section 12). An agreement label has no registry line, so until 17:45 such a replaced circuit was recorded nowhere except the git history and the run's key/labels-before.jsonl, and a later run that wrote it again sent it to a judge again (improvements-ideas.md). Since 17:45 judge-publish.mjs keeps it in <tag>-replaced-agreement.jsonl; for the random benchmark that file was added afterwards with 306 lines. The one agreement label it lacks is the round-2 label of "the first paragraph removed", whose circuit is still recorded only in git and labels-before.jsonl. Always read L9 and L10 against the frozen labels, and do not delete key/labels-before.jsonl.

drop-duplicates.mjs --apply would now remove the kept circuits

checkRegistryLines in tests/benchmark/syntax/drop-duplicates.mjs removes a line with judge "agreement" whenever the message's cell holds a label with accepted_by judge or written, whatever circuit that label is. The lines of a replaced-agreement file are exactly of that kind. Run at 18:00 without --apply, the tool reported: "306 agreement lines repeat the syntactic files and can be removed: 0 files rewritten, 1 files left with no line and removed." With --apply it would delete 2026-11-27-benchmark-random-replaced-agreement.jsonl. Do not run it with --apply until that rule is changed.

The rules in a run folder are the latest, not those the verdicts were given under

Every call of judge-prepare.mjs, round.mjs settle (both through writeJudging in lib.mjs), round.mjs rewrite-prepare or pool.mjs collect copies rules.md over the folder's RULES.md (copyFileSync). This has just happened: the benchmark's judging/RULES.md (17:49) holds the rules with 29 settled cases, while the 5,858 verdicts of the first pass were given at 16:25–16:38 under an earlier version. To know the rules behind a verdict file, look up tests/benchmark/syntax/rules.md in git at the time of the verdicts.

Benchmark messages in training data

The owner allows training on the dataset, its texts and its accepted circuits, but not hard-coding its cases (owner's decision). The 1% benchmark is therefore not a held-out set for a learned strategy unless training excludes it, and the table has to be read with that in mind:

  • The DistilBERT checkpoint .local-models/distilbert/syntax-v1-silver was trained with eval-results/conversion/silver-vss-spacy.jsonl (58,103 agreement circuits, weight 0.3, nothing excluded per its training-summary.json, 16:19:23). That file holds 576 of the 1,831 benchmark messages. Which checkpoint wrote the judged circuits of first-run/distilbert.jsonl is not recorded. They are not the circuits of gold-only/distilbert.jsonl (1,137 of 1,831 differ); the packets that hold them were written at 16:20:06, after the silver checkpoint's training summary; and the split of 99.3% on those 576 messages against 59.2% on the other 1,255 (section 7) fits a model trained on the agreement circuits. Likely, not verified.
  • strategies/distilbert/retrain.mjs now reads every label of the syntactic files: judge and written labels as gold, agreement labels as silver unless --no-agreement. 1,745 benchmark messages carry a label now (1,482 judge, 263 agreement), so a retrain without --exclude trains on them. The checkpoint syntax-v2 (17:38) excluded them: its training-summary.json lists 1,482 gold and 263 agreement lines excluded. --exclude takes a plain JSON array or JSON Lines file of {text}. The file is read as UTF-8 text, so the gzipped sample must be unpacked first.
  • VSS learns from the text column of the whole dataset, benchmark messages included (strategies/vss/train.mjs). That is learning from text, not from labels.
  • strategies/distilbert.md still names labels.jsonl as the default gold file. The file was removed, and retrain.mjs refuses --gold.

Labels count without a judge

A circuit equal to the label is L9 and is never judged. Where labels were made by agreement of two strategies, those two strategies collect L9 on every such message. On the random benchmark that is 576 messages, all of them L9 for VSS (section 7). Report GOAL together with the split by labelled and unlabelled messages when comparing strategies.

Small files, and rules drawn from the files measured

VSS had 83.7% and 86.8% on the first runs of the small files of rounds 1 and 2, but 65.9% on the random sample (recounted in section 8). improvements-ideas.md gives the reason that the dataset is dominated by long drawing and chart messages; on the random sample, though, the drawing and diagram messages are not longer than the others (13.9 and 12.4 words on average against 13.1), and VSS is weakest on diagram messages (syntax-work-2026-11-27.html, I3). Rules drawn from a round's own files inflate that round's score: VSS went from 83.7% to 92.3% on round 1 after the first rules from its rejections (the round's report.md), and to 93.4% with the file now in the folder. Only unseen files measure.

Retraining VSS shifts unrelated readings

The learned model of VSS is estimated again from scratch after any change to strategies/vss/legend.sop or the word-form rules in strategies/vss/src/forms.mjs, so readings that have nothing to do with the change can shift. Marks are therefore added as new open lists, the model is retrained once, and the damage is checked at once. A change to the stem rule for "goes" was dropped for this reason (session notes; no count recorded). Counting word kinds (verb, thing, quality) from the accepted circuits as well changed 41 of 1,831 benchmark circuits with no net gain (improvements-ideas.md, "Measured and dropped"; not recounted here).

Judging cost

  • Judging every strategy's circuit in round 1 meant 1,798 circuits for 575 messages (settle.json). Reading only the circuit the conversion needs (--judge needed) brought a round down to a few packets (session notes, syntax-work-2026-11-27.html, I10; not counted). The benchmark judged 5,858 circuits for 1,831 messages, because every strategy needs a level there.
  • The batch put a whole dataset's audit into 11 pooled packets (1,718 lines) instead of one judge per folder for 217 folders (pool.mjs).
  • The table of all strategies comes from the 1% benchmark, not from every round (owner's decision).

Smaller traps

  • A verdict file with one malformed line blocks the whole publication. Run check.mjs on every packet first.
  • run.mjs without --sample runs the earlier fixed sample, on which strategies were tuned.
  • round.mjs start finds no file now (section 8); continue through the existing folders.
  • round.mjs write without --judge writes "unknown" into the registry lines without a warning.
  • report.md in a round folder is a snapshot; written.json records what was published, but the written.json files of the rounds and of the batch name agreement lines that were later removed from the registry.
  • A run folder can be reused by later reruns, as the benchmark folder was. Keep the judged strategy files apart (first-run/) before rerunning.

17. What is not measured

  • How often two judges give the same verdict on the same circuit: not measured. Each circuit was read by one judge. The settled cases in rules.md were added where judges ruled differently, which shows that disagreement exists.
  • How often the language-model judge agrees with a human annotator: not measured (no such measurement is recorded in the repository or cited in the session notes).
  • How accurate the circuits written by judges are: not measured. They are accepted without a second reading.
  • How reliable agreement is for pairs involving DistilBERT: 23 audited circuits, none rejected, too few to say; and the checkpoint behind them is not recorded.
  • Whether agreements matched only under the relaxed comparison are less reliable than exact ones: 4 of 270 against 6 of 870 audited, too few to say.
  • How far GOAL on the random 1% carries over to messages from outside the dataset: not measured.
  • How often normalForm falls back to the circuit as written, and whether such circuits fail to match equal readings: not measured.

18. Sources, and the owner's decisions

The owner's decisions of 2026-11-27

The working session of 2026-11-27 kept these decisions, with their reasons, in notes that are not in the repository. syntax-work-2026-11-27.html records the same decisions as D1 to D14, with the commits where each landed; the plan and several of the measurements are also in improvements-ideas.md. This document refers to them by content and links here.

  1. The goal is judged acceptance: GOAL = L8 + L9 + L10, ideally over 99%. It is not restated as precision plus coverage; instead the dataset is corrected through the judged loop until the comparison with it is exact.
  2. Fair judging, the external reviewer's criterion adopted by the owner: a circuit passes if the next layer can continue from it by completing it, and fails only when it states something wrong or loses explicit information.
  3. A small, purely syntactic schema (syntax, version 3, four wire types, 18 keys, established grammar terms) replaces the 50-key nlu schema.
  4. Every strategy writes the syntax schema directly from the text, with no knowledge repository and no use of the old annotations, which are no longer trusted.
  5. Rules and word lists may name only function words and common verbs, never nouns, adjectives or anything particular to the dataset. Training on the dataset (its text and its accepted circuits) is allowed; hard-coding its cases is not.
  6. Evaluation on a random 1% of the whole dataset (1,831 messages). Every circuit a judge accepts becomes the label of its message, whatever the message had before.
  7. The dataset is split into semantic/ and syntactic/, kept as mirrors, with no file over 1,000 messages.
  8. Testing and converting go together in rounds. A round takes the next source files without a syntactic file, and a file under 500 messages means taking another.
  9. One judge and one fixer in batch: per message a judge reads one circuit, and a fixer writes the circuit when it is rejected. The table of all strategies comes from the 1% benchmark, not from every round.
  10. At most four strategies are worked on, chosen for different sources of analysis: vss, spacy, distilbert and one hand-written grammar. Prolog and Z3 are parked, and eight close variants are left out.
  11. DistilBERT is retrained on syntax circuits only, from the pretrained base model.
  12. No duplicated data: the syntactic files are the only store of labels, and the registry keeps only what a judge read or wrote.
  13. The partial semantic work of an earlier session was committed as it stood, for the history.
  14. Commit each step, keep the plan in improvements-ideas.md, and run independent work in parallel.
